# Fee for Service

## Summary

Fee-for-service pays for each delivered unit of care and remains the dominant payment baseline because it is straightforward and deeply embedded in contracts, billing systems, workflows, and revenue expectations. Value-based models add quality, savings, risk, or population accountability, but many providers now operate under both approaches and face conflicting volume and value incentives.

## Why Fee-for-Service Persists

Fee-for-service is easy to understand and administer: a defined service produces a defined payment. Its contracts, billing systems, data flows, and organizational economics have developed over decades, so moving away from it requires investment and changes to operations as well as a different contract.

## The Value-Based Transition – Challenges

Moving toward value-based care changes incentives and expands accountability. Stakeholders must agree on the population, outcomes, cost measure, and time horizon, while providers need scale, leadership, clinical alignment, and data capabilities to manage the added responsibility.

## The Current Landscape – A Hybrid Model

Providers commonly operate in a hybrid system, described in the course as “straddling two canoes.” Fee-for-service rewards additional volume, while value-based contracts may reward prevention, coordination, lower total cost, and measured quality. The same organization can therefore be pushed in different directions.

## Government Influence – A Key Driver

Government has used its role as a major sponsor to pilot and spread alternative payment models. The deck points to CMMI, the Medicare Shared Savings Program, accountable care organizations, and newer episode- and population-based models, while also showing that the overall move away from fee-for-service has been slow.

## Limits of These Sources

These materials describe payment categories and adoption patterns but do not prove that a particular contract improved quality or lowered total cost. Results depend on contract details, organizational capability, patient mix, and the measures selected.
